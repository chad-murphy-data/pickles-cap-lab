A few good questions.

The rules, the ratings, and how the player prices work.

How does this work?

Build a six-player team with three women and three men, spending between $500,000 and $1 million in assigned player values. At least four players participate in a match.

There are two ways to play:

  • Build your dream team: Choose from the full player pool, swap players whenever you like, and get a team score out of 100.
  • Draft against the computer: Share the player pool with 19 computer teams. Each player can belong to only one team. Finish the draft and see how your team compares.

There’s no reward for saving money. The goal is to build the strongest team.

Where do the numbers come from?

The starting point is PICKLES player ratings, using a snapshot from October 7, 2026. Those ratings estimate doubles and singles ability from recorded results.

I use those ratings to calculate how different lineups would perform in an MLP-style match, including women’s doubles, men’s doubles, two mixed doubles games, and a DreamBreaker if doubles finishes 2–2.

The prices are values assigned for this game. They aren’t salaries, contract estimates, or predictions of what an owner would actually pay.

How did you arrive at valuations for the players?

I measured how much a player improves a team when replacing another player, repeated that comparison across many lineups, and converted the average improvement into a price.

Each version faces the same six reference opponent profiles. Those opponents represent different team strengths and compositions.

That improvement is what I mean by contribution: how much more often the team is expected to win when this player fills the slot.

Repeat with different teammates. Every man gets the same 540 tests: 180 source rosters, testing each of their three men’s slots. Women get the corresponding 540 women’s tests.

In each test, I compare the player with the strongest replacement option for that lineup. A test where the player doesn’t improve the team earns zero contribution. I then average the improvements.

Across all those tests, Ben’s average contribution was 25.70 percentage points. CJ’s was 8.03 percentage points.

Finally, turn contribution into dollars. This is an explicit pricing choice: the simulations don’t discover a dollar exchange rate.

I chose one common conversion that puts Waters, the highest contributor, at $775,000. That leaves enough for five $45,000 players. The same conversion applies to everyone: approximately $15,179 per percentage point of contribution, plus a $45,000 roster baseline. Performance premiums round down to $5,000 increments.

The full chain is:

Ratings → predicted match outcomes → improvement from changing one player → average improvement across many lineups → dollar conversion → price.

Why are the top women more expensive than the top men?

Every team needs three women and three men, so an available man can’t fill an open women’s slot.

In this ratings snapshot, the strongest women generally create larger improvements over the available replacement women than the strongest men create over replacement men. The men’s pool has more players with relatively similar contributions, giving teams more alternatives.

That makes the top women more expensive under a shared dollar conversion. It isn’t a claim that women’s and men’s PICKLES ratings are directly comparable—they use separate rating scales—or that a particular woman would beat a particular man.

It also depends on this player pool and format. Change the roster requirements, available players, or ratings, and the prices could change.

Is a roster with ALW plus five minimum-price players actually viable?

Waters is exceptionally strong, and the cap also limits the teams she faces.

An opposing team can’t simply assemble a collection of stars to overwhelm her supporting players. Everyone has the same $1 million limit and the same roster requirements.

At $775,000, Waters leaves exactly $225,000 for five $45,000 players. That creates a viable strategy, but choosing those five still matters. Their ratings differ, and they need to provide useful doubles partnerships and DreamBreaker options.

The model allows a Waters-centered team to be strong. It doesn’t guarantee that every Waters team will be the strongest.

What is a match?

It mirrors MLP matches, where each match includes women’s doubles, men’s doubles, and two mixed doubles games. If the teams finish those four games tied at 2–2, a DreamBreaker decides the winner.

The model selects doubles pairings and DreamBreaker participants from each six-player roster using their ratings. It accounts for both partners’ ratings in doubles, but doesn’t separately model every partnership, preferred side, or chemistry advantage.

How much does DreamBreaker ability matter?

DreamBreaker ability affects the predicted result only when the four doubles games finish 2–2.

The model uses four-point rotations, with two men followed by two women, and accounts for the stopping rule. Players don’t automatically receive equal numbers of rallies.

To explain the singles portion of a price, I also compare a player’s singles rating with the singles ability expected from someone of their doubles level. That separates the value associated with their general playing level from the adjustment for being unusually strong or weak in singles.

DreamBreakers can matter more in a balanced league because closely matched teams have more opportunities to finish doubles at 2–2. Even so, singles estimates deserve caution when a player has limited recent data.

Why don’t some players have a singles rank?

I withhold precise singles ranks for players without recorded 2026 singles games in this snapshot.

That avoids presenting an uncertain estimate as a current competitive ranking. The match model can still use their estimated singles ability, including information from recorded DreamBreaker play.

What does the $45,000 minimum price represent?

Every player’s price starts with a $45,000 roster-slot baseline, with a performance premium added based on the contribution tests described above.

Players at the minimum price can still be valuable additions to your team. Their doubles and singles strengths differ, and finding the right fit for your roster matters.

The minimum groups together players whose measured contributions don’t produce an additional premium after rounding. It doesn’t mean they have identical abilities.

Should similarly rated players have similar prices?

Yes. Within each gender, the same doubles and singles ratings produce the same price.

Every player is evaluated in the same set of test lineups. A player doesn’t get a discount because their name happened to fit poorly into one particular simulated roster.

Confidence is shown separately rather than used to create a hidden price discount.

What does my team’s score mean?

In Build your dream team, the 0–100 score compares your team’s predicted performance with the weakest and strongest qualifying teams found during the benchmark searches.

It is not your percentage chance of winning a match, and 100 doesn’t mean unbeatable. It means reaching the strongest benchmark found.

In Draft against the computer, your projected ranking compares your completed team with the other 19 drafted teams. Any displayed expected win percentage refers to match performance against that field, not your chance of winning a championship.

These are model estimates. They don’t account for everything that can happen on court.

Why can’t I select a particular player?

The player may already have been drafted, your team may already have three players of that gender, or you may not have enough budget left.

The game reserves at least $45,000 for every remaining roster slot, so you can finish your team.

The selectable pool is limited to the top 100 players of each gender by doubles rating in the snapshot.

How does the computer draft?

The first round starts with the highest-priced affordable player remaining, giving each team a player to build around.

Later picks use a search for players who strengthen that team while preserving its budget and roster requirements.

The computer can see the available players and completed picks. It can’t see whom you intend to choose next.

Is this an official MLP game or a prediction of next season?

No. This is an unofficial fan project using MLP-style roster rules to create a fantasy pickleball experience.

It’s a way to explore team combinations, test your knowledge, and imagine a league built under a value cap. It doesn’t determine future eligibility or account for current contracts and player availability.
